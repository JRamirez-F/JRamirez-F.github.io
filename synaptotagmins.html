The Synaptic Vesicle Cycle

Synaptic vesicle (SV) exocytosis is the fundamental mechanism by which chemical synapses transmit information. Action potentials open voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels, producing highly localized elevations in intracellular Ca²⁺ that trigger vesicle fusion through the coordinated action of SNARE proteins and their regulators [Südhof, 2004; Südhof, 2013]. To maintain neurotransmission during repetitive activity, presynaptic terminals must not only release neurotransmitters with remarkable temporal precision but also recover and replenish functional vesicles.

After full-collapse fusion, vesicular membrane proteins are incorporated into the presynaptic plasma membrane and become exposed to the extracellular environment. Their retrieval, sorting and reuse must be coordinated with endocytosis to maintain the molecular composition of synaptic vesicles [Chanaday et al., 2019; Chanaday & Kavalali, 2017; Opazo et al., 2010]. How synaptic vesicle proteins remain coordinated and rapidly reusable after exocytosis, particularly during high-frequency activity, remains an important question in synaptic neuroscience.

Synaptotagmins: Beyond Calcium Sensing

Synaptotagmins (SYTs) are a family of membrane proteins with diverse functions in regulated secretion. SYT1 and SYT2 are major Ca²⁺ sensors for fast synchronous neurotransmitter release. Their modular architecture comprises an N-terminal luminal domain, a single transmembrane helix, a flexible cytoplasmic linker and two cytoplasmic C2 domains (C2A and C2B), which participate in Ca²⁺-dependent interactions with lipids and the fusion machinery [Park & Ryu, 2018; Koh & Bellen, 2003; Geppert et al., 1994].

Beyond their classical role in triggering vesicle fusion, synaptotagmins participate in the coordination of exocytosis and endocytosis [Chen et al., 2022; Bolz et al., 2023]. Across the 17 mammalian synaptotagmin isoforms, the cytoplasmic C2 domains are relatively conserved, whereas the N-terminal regions display substantial sequence variability.

The N-terminal region is exposed to the extracellular space upon vesicle fusion. Of particular interest is the luminal/extracellular juxtamembrane domain (JMD), immediately adjacent to the transmembrane helix, which provides a molecular interface for interactions at the membrane surface.

Synaptotagmin–Ganglioside Interactions

Our previous work, initially motivated by the molecular recognition of synaptotagmin by botulinum neurotoxin type B (BoNT/B), identified a ganglioside-binding motif in the luminal/extracellular JMD of SYT1 and SYT2. We showed that binding of the polysialoganglioside GT1b promotes a disorder-to-α-helix transition in this region, leading to the formation of a high-affinity co-receptor for BoNT/B [Flores & Ramírez-Franco et al., 2019; Ramírez-Franco et al., 2022].

In a subsequent study, we identified a naturally occurring amino acid substitution (N59Q) in the extracellular JMD of SYT2 in members of the Felidae family and showed that it prevents BoNT/B binding [Ramírez-Franco et al., 2022]. This finding demonstrates that a single sequence variation in this region can profoundly alter toxin recognition.

An evolutionary paradox

Despite the substantial diversity of N-terminal sequences across the synaptotagmin family, the luminal/extracellular JMDs of SYT1 and SYT2 are strikingly conserved between these two paralogs and across vertebrate orthologs. This is particularly intriguing given that both isoforms are major sensors for fast synchronous neurotransmission.

Why has a domain that confers susceptibility to a potent bacterial neurotoxin remained so highly conserved? One plausible explanation is that this region is subject to evolutionary constraints arising from an important physiological function. This possibility shifts the focus from toxin recognition to the endogenous role of SYT1/2–ganglioside interactions in the organization and recycling of synaptic vesicle proteins.

Preserving Synaptic Vesicle Identity

Following full-collapse exocytosis, vesicle proteins could disperse laterally within the presynaptic plasma membrane. Nevertheless, the retrieval and reuse of vesicular components must occur efficiently enough to support repeated rounds of release. Studies of vesicle protein organization and mobility indicate that diffusion, clustering and cargo retrieval are important aspects of this process [Opazo et al., 2010; Rizzoli, 2014; Willig et al., 2006; Gimber et al., 2015].

One particularly intriguing feature of synaptotagmin is the presence of a fraction of the protein at the plasma membrane, outside the vesicular pool [Rizzoli, 2014]. Given the ability of SYT1/2 to interact with gangliosides, this resident population provides a starting point for investigating a possible mechanism of protein confinement at the presynaptic surface.

Working hypothesis. Interactions between the extracellular JMDs of SYT1/2 and GT1b may contribute to diffusional trapping of newly exocytosed vesicular proteins, restricting their lateral dispersion and facilitating their retrieval for subsequent rounds of neurotransmission.

This model predicts that disturbing JMD-dependent interactions could alter vesicle protein mobility, the kinetics of the exo–endocytic cycle, vesicle pool organization and, ultimately, the ability of synapses to sustain neurotransmitter release during repetitive activity. Whether these predictions hold remains to be tested.

Selected methodological approaches

The following experimental framework was developed to investigate the physiological role of the SYT1/2 extracellular domains. It is included here as scientific context for the hypothesis, not as a description of ongoing experiments or a commitment to carry them out.

01 — Activity-dependent extracellular interactions

Proximity-labeling proteomics in neuronal cultures could characterize the molecular environment of the exposed SYT1/2 luminal domains during vesicle exocytosis. Comparing resting and stimulated conditions, with and without perturbation of ganglioside synthesis, could identify candidate proximity partners for validation by direct interaction assays [Pascual-Caro & de Juan-Sanz, 2024].

02 — Vesicle dynamics and presynaptic organization

Live-cell fluorescence imaging with pHluorin-tagged vesicle proteins, combined with genetic and lipid perturbations, could assess exo–endocytosis kinetics and protein spreading. Electron microscopy could complement these experiments by examining vesicle pool organization and redistribution [Ramírez-Franco et al., 2014; Bolz et al., 2023; Gimber et al., 2015; Sankaranarayanan et al., 2000; Chang et al., 2018].

03 — Functional consequences for neurotransmission

Electrophysiological recordings in autaptic hippocampal cultures could establish whether perturbing JMD-dependent interactions affects spontaneous and evoked release, the readily releasable pool and short-term plasticity during repetitive activity [Bouazza-Arostegui et al., 2022].

04 — Nanoscale structure and dynamics

High-speed atomic force microscopy (HS-AFM) and force-spectroscopy AFM in defined membrane systems could probe how gangliosides influence synaptotagmin organization, mobility and molecular interactions [Longarzo et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024].
